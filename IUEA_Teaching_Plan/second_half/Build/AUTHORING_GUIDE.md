## The course situation (do not re-decide)
- Year 3 Engineering undergraduates at IUEA, three cohorts, three hours per class, once a week. They are NEW to programming. Weeks 1 to 7 taught Python, NumPy, pandas, cleaning and charts (Practical Test 1 was Week 7). They learn machine learning in the next four classes. The semester then ends (Week 12 is a project clinic; the project is submitted, take-home, and counts 40 percent of the course; the Week 8 to 11 assignments and quizzes are practice that does not count). Other advanced topics move to next semester.
- Cohorts: CML3112 (Civil and Architecture, Tuesday, building cooling load, kW), CML3113 (Mining and Petroleum, Friday 08:00, crusher energy demand, kWh/t), CML3111 (Electrical, Mechatronics, Robotics, Communications, Friday 11:00, motor winding temperature, deg C). Same data structure (engineering_data.csv, 91 rows, E1 to E6 equipment groups, load_pct, ambient_c, target). One class file serves all three: `build(code)` returns the class dict, numbers come from `common.facts(code)` and the NumPy reference module, never typed.
- Classes: 1 your first model (done), 2 classification (Week 9), 3 can I trust this model (Week 10), 4 features and choosing a model plus the project brief (Week 11). Block 1 = Classes 1 and 2, Block 2 = Classes 3 and 4. A quiz sitting at the end of Class 2 and Class 4. Assignments are separate and are not your job.
- The lecturer's non-negotiable: **concepts first, lab second.** The deck has a concepts half (no laptops, diagrams, plain words, code only as a read-aloud 'recipe') then a 'Part 2' divider slide, then a few lab slides, then the notebook is where the lab happens. He must switch from slides to notebook ONCE. Every term is explained in plain words before first use, with a picture. Every slide has a drawn figure or icons (at most one text-only slide in five). Titles state the point. Speaker notes are a first-person spoken script of 60 to 130 words, ending on a question where it fits.
- House style: no 'X, not Y' constructions, no em dashes or en dashes, no 'honestly', 'genuinely', 'straightforward', 'journey', 'leverage', 'delve', no he or she for learners (address them as you), no stage directions in notes, hedge anything legal or uncertain ('my understanding is', 'check with your lawyer'). No invented statistics. Numbers from the reference. Label invented teaching numbers as invented/illustrative on the slide.
- Friday cohorts had the CLASS 1 THEORY already but no lab (Class 1). For Classes 2 to 4 all three cohorts are on the same footing: same deck, same clock (the 'friday' flag is only for Class 1).

## Concept sequence rules
Start each class by returning to what the class before taught (recall slide, three questions), say in one slide why today matters in engineering terms for the cohort, introduce each idea with a plain-words definition, one picture and one tiny example you can do by hand, then a bridge slide before the divider that shows the code recipe in four to six lines with a plain-words reading (kind 'code'). Finish the concepts with a one-slide summary before the break/divider. The lab slides after the divider: a lab map (steps), 'check your numbers' table, an error-cards table, and a short 'how this class counts' slide.

## Revision pass (2 Nov 2026, after the lecturer reviewed the decks): SELF-STUDY CLARITY AND FORMULAS
The decks will be shared as PDFs and students will revise from them alone, WITHOUT the speaker notes. So every slide must be understandable on its own:
- Write full, plain sentences, not telegraphic fragments. Define a term on the slide where it first appears. Give the unit. Say what the picture shows ('Each dot is one training row', 'The red segments are the errors').
- Hunt ambiguity: any phrase a beginner could read two ways, any pronoun with no clear noun, any 'it', 'them', 'those' that could mean several things, any jargon used before it is defined, odd or unnatural wording. Real examples the lecturer flagged (fix these and all like them):
  1. Title 'A model cannot learn from a blank, so we fill it from the training rows' (a blank what?) -> say 'missing value (an empty cell)' and explain: 'A model cannot use an empty cell. We fill each empty input cell with the median of that column, taken from the training rows only.'
  2. 'Each box at the bottom predicts the average target of the training rows that land in it.' -> 'The yellow boxes at the bottom are the answers. A row follows the yes or no answers down the tree until it reaches one yellow box. The prediction is the average target of the training rows that ended in that same box.'
  3. 'max_depth limits how many questions in a row. Deeper trees can memorise: we test that in Class 3.' -> 'max_depth is the largest number of questions a row can be asked on the way down. This tree has depth 2: two questions. A deeper tree asks more questions and can fit the training rows more closely, even to the point of memorising them. Class 3 tests that.'
- You may raise slide word counts: lint limits are now idea 100, steps 100, table 150, formula 120 words. Prefer a second slide to cramming. Keep text 18 pt or larger on the slide (tables 14 pt minimum). Never shrink the picture to make room.
- Recall slides: the expected answers must also be readable, so make the recall slide show question AND a short model answer (use the existing recall mechanism but check how it renders; if answers are only in the notes, add a slide `kind: table` with columns Question / Short answer immediately after).
- Fix every typo and odd phrasing; check the lab notebooks the same way (markdown text must be clear to a beginner; the PREDICT/READ/CHANGE prompts must be unambiguous).
- Keep the class's order and the concepts-then-lab structure. The notes (spoken script) should still match the slide after your edits.

FORMULAS (industry standard, with every symbol named). Add the maths where it helps, using the new slide kind 'formula': `dict(kind='formula', title=..., formulas=[(label, r'$...$'), ...], symbols=[[symbol, meaning, 'in our data'], ...], note=..., notes=...)`. Formulas are mathtext strings (see kit/diagrams_ml.py x_math; tall lines with \frac or \sum need ~1.3 in of height, so at most 3 formula lines per slide with a symbol table of at most 6 rows under them). Rules: every symbol is named in the table, with its meaning in plain words and where it appears in OUR data (column name, unit, number); show a worked numerical example from the cohort's own numbers (from the reference) on the same slide or the next; subscripts index rows or features and say so. Industry-standard notation: y for the actual target, y-hat for the prediction, x_1, x_2 for features, b_0 for the intercept and b_1, b_2 for weights (coefficients), n for the number of rows, e_i = y_i - y-hat_i, MAE, MSE, RMSE as the usual sums, p for probability, TP FP FN TN, precision, recall, F1, accuracy, mu and sigma for the training mean and spread, k-fold mean and standard deviation, ridge loss with alpha. Put formulas for what the class teaches; do not teach maths beyond the class. Name the real learned numbers (e.g. intercept 1.49, weight for load_pct 0.447, weight for ambient_c 0.756 for CML3112) and say what a one-unit change in a feature does to the prediction.
